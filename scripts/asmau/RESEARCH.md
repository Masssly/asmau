# Nana Asma'u: grounding notes

Working notes for the game about Nana Asma'u bint Usman dan Fodio (about 1793 to 1864), the person this
site is named after. Unlike the prophets' stories there is no Qur'an text to check against, so every fact
in the game must come from the sources below, and the game says which. Fetched 14 Nov 2026.

## Sources, strongest first
- **Boyd & Mack, *The Collected Works of Nana Asma'u, Daughter of Usman dan Fodiyo (1793-1864)*, Michigan
  State University Press, 1997.** Her own poems in translation, with an introduction and "An Outline
  Chronology" (marked * where the date is tentative). The chronology rests largely on Waziri Junaidu (1957),
  the Sokoto historian. Referred to below as **CW**.
- Jean Boyd, *The Caliph's Sister* (1989); Mack & Boyd, *One Woman's Jihad* (2000); Boyd & Mack,
  *Educating Muslim Women* (2013). Not read for this; cited by the sources above.
- Wikipedia, "Nana Asmaʾu" (read 14 Nov 2026). Useful summary; it disagrees with other sources on the date the
  women teachers' network began (see below).
- Yaqeen Institute, "A Plea to Saintly Women: The Life and Legacy of Nana Asma'u".
- Sahih al-Bukhari 2979 and 3907 (sunnah.com): Asma bint Abi Bakr and the two belts.

## Facts the game may use (source)
- Born about 1793 in Degel, north-west of Sokoto, a twin; her brother was Hassan. (CW chronology; CW
  intro, from Waziri Junaidu.) The birth year is worked out from her age at death, so "about 1793".
- Twins were customarily named Hassan and Hussain; she would have been Hussaina, but her father, the Shehu
  Usman dan Fodio, named her Asma'u, after Asma bint Abi Bakr, a Companion of the Prophet. (CW intro; Wikipedia.)
- Asma bint Abi Bakr: when the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr left Makkah for Madinah, she prepared their food and
  water, and, having nothing to tie them with, cut her waistband in two, one piece for the water-skin and one
  for the food; so she was called "the one with the two belts" (Dhat an-Nitaqayn). (Bukhari 2979, 3907.)
- Her mother died when she was small (CW: 1795, tentative). She was brought up by the Shehu's wives Aisha and
  Hauwa, Muhammad Bello's mother. (CW chronology.)
- She learned the Qur'an by heart at a young age. (CW intro.) Children learned to write on wooden boards
  (allo) (a later popular source; widely known practice in the region; the game says "like children there do").
- 21 February 1804: the Shehu's hijra (emigration) from Degel to Gudu, the start of the jihad; she was about
  eleven. Years of war followed (1804-1808), including defeats with heavy losses (Tsuntsuwa, 1804) and the
  taking of Alkalawa in 1808. (CW chronology.) She later wrote of these years: "The Journey" and "The Song of
  Wandering" (CW; Wikipedia).
- In those years women organised food for the army, brought up orphans, gave out goods to the poor, and
  taught. (CW intro, on the women of the community.)
- 1807 she married Usman Gidado, a close friend of her brother Muhammad Bello; 1809 she went to live in
  Sokoto, the new town built by Bello. First son Abdulkadir about 1810. (CW chronology.) She ran a household of
  several hundred people, seeing to food, clothing and shelter. (CW intro.)
- 20 April 1817 her father died; her twin Hassan died that November. She and Gidado then collected and had
  copied all the Shehu's papers. (CW chronology.)
- 1820: her first written work, The Way of the Pious. (CW.) She wrote in Fulfulde, Hausa and Arabic, using
  Arabic letters for all three (ajami), so that people who did not know Arabic could learn. (Wikipedia; CW.)
  She knew four languages: Arabic, Fulfulde, Hausa and Tamasheq. (Wikipedia; other sources.)
- 1829: "The Qur'an", a poem naming every sura of the Qur'an, made to be easy to remember. (CW chronology
  and intro; Yaqeen.)
- More than 60 surviving works over about 40 years. (Wikipedia.)
- The women teachers: she trained women leaders called jaji, who came to her to learn and then taught women
  in their homes and villages, mostly through poems that were easy to remember; their groups were the
  'Yan Taru, "those who come together", the sisterhood. Each jaji wore a tall hat (malfa) as a sign of her
  office. (CW; Wikipedia.) DATES DIFFER: Wikipedia says from about 1830; another summary says 1840. The game
  gives no year. The 'Yan Taru still meet today. (Wikipedia; CW field notes.)
- She was known as "Uwar Gari", mother of the town, from about the 1830s. (CW.)
- She wrote elegies for those she lost, among them her brother Bello, her husband Gidado, and her close friend
  Aisha. (CW.)
- She died in 1864, aged about 70 (dates differ by a year in the sources). Schools, women's groups and a
  university in northern Nigeria are named after her. (Wikipedia.)

## What the game will NOT say, and why
- The battles in detail, or anything about captives and enslavement (CW and Wikipedia say her teaching network
  partly began to bring captured women into the community). Parents' notes say so plainly.
- "The miracle of the burning branch" (CW chronology, 1808): a story, not established fact; left out.
- Any quotation of her poems longer than a short phrase: the translations are under copyright.
- No language lessons in Fulfulde, Hausa or Tamasheq words: not checked by a speaker.
