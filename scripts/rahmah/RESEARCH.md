## Facts for the story

- Born in 1900 in Bukit Surungan, Padang Panjang, West Sumatra, then the Dutch East Indies; youngest child of a
  Minangkabau family of Islamic scholars. Her father, Muhammad Yunus, was a qadi (judge); her mother was Rafi'ah. Her
  grandfather was a scholar and astronomer. [W]
- Her father died when she was six. His former students taught her; she learned to read and write in Arabic and Latin
  script. She also had some training in midwifery at a local hospital. [W]
- Married in 1916 to a scholar, Bahauddin Lathif, and made to leave school; kept studying in private study circles from
  1918. In 1922 her husband took two more wives; she obtained a divorce and went back to school. No children. [W]
- Her brother Zainuddin Labay el Yunusi had founded the Diniyah School in 1915 (mixed). Back there, she led study
  sessions for the girls outside class. [W] The Minangkabau are matrilineal, yet girls had little access to religious
  education: the paradox she set out to fix. [F]
- **1 November 1923:** she opened Diniyah Putri (Madrasah Diniyah li al-Banat), generally thought the first Islamic
  school for girls in the Indies [W]; "the first Islamic educational institution exclusively for women in Southeast
  Asia" [F]. It began in a mosque, with her as the main teacher; the first 71 students were mostly young housewives;
  lessons in Islam, Arabic grammar, some modern subjects and handicrafts. [W] Later also health, sewing, leadership,
  English, history, geography. [F]
- Not everyone accepted it: she faced hostility and criticism [W], from those who rejected formal education for
  women [F]. She won over local scholars "through communication strategies and a religious approach". [F]
- 1924: a classroom in a house; her brother Zainuddin died the same year, and she carried on. [W]
- **1926:** the Padang Panjang earthquakes destroyed the school building; classes met in makeshift buildings for years.
  She declined Muhammadiyah's offer to take the school over, toured the Indies to raise money, and a new building opened
  in 1928. [W]
- She refused Dutch government subsidies for her schools. [W] By the late 1930s about 500 students. Audrey Kahin calls
  Diniyah Putri "one of the most successful and influential of the schools for women". [W] Teacher training institute,
  Kulliyatul Muallimat al-Islamiyyah, 1937; schools in Jakarta 1935; a high school 1938. [W]
- **1945:** on hearing of Indonesia's independence she raised the red-and-white flag in the school yard. [W]
- **Al-Azhar:** the Grand Imam of al-Azhar, Abd al-Rahman Taj, visited the school (1956 [W]; 1955 in other sources);
  in 1957, after her Hajj, she was invited to al-Azhar, whose faculty gave her the title Syaikhah, never before given to
  a woman [W] (the 2026 article gives 1955 [F]). Diniyah Putri graduates then received scholarships to al-Azhar. [W]
- 1967: a women's Islamic university, which she had pushed for, was opened. She died in Padang Panjang on 26 February
  1969; her grave is in the school grounds. In November 2025 she was named a National Hero of Indonesia. [W] The school
  is still open (Diniyyah Puteri). [W]

## For the parents' notes only (owner's rule: hard history goes there)

- Her marriage at about sixteen, her husband's two further marriages, the divorce. [W]
- The Japanese occupation: she led a Giyugun (local defence) unit, and campaigned against the Japanese use of
  Indonesian women as "comfort women". [W]
- Seven months in a Dutch prison in 1949. [W]
- Politics: elected to parliament in 1955 for the Masyumi party, one of the first women; her support for the PRRI
  rebellion (1958) cost her the seat; arrested 1961, freed by amnesty. Her disagreement with her former student and
  teacher Rasuna Said. [W]

## Open questions (settle before release)

- Her birth date: 26 October 1900 [W]; 20 December 1900 (Alfian, per search summary) or 29 December 1900 (other
  Indonesian sources). The game says only "1900".
- The year of the Syaikhah title: 1957 [W] or 1955 [F]. The game can say "after her Hajj" without a year.
- Do not use quotations attributed to her until one is found in a named source.
